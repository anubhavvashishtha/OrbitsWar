1. The "95% Send" Suicide
We just implemented a rule to send 95% of available ships to maximize fleet speed.

The Exploit: A smart enemy will send a tiny, fast 1-ship fleet to attack your massive Tank. Your Tank will ignore it, launch 95% of its ships at an enemy target, and leave only a 5% reserve. One turn later, the enemy's 1-ship fleet lands, effortlessly captures your now-empty Tank, and steals all its massive production capacity.
The Reality: Sending 95% of your ships makes you incredibly fast, but it leaves your planets completely naked to counter-attacks.
2. The Quadrant System is Arbitrary
You divide the map into strict mathematical quadrants (top-left, top-right, etc.).

The Exploit: If you have a dying planet at coordinates (1, 1) (Quadrant 0), and a massive friendly Tank at (-1, 1) (Quadrant 1), they are physically right next to each other. However, because they are in different mathematical quadrants, your Tank will completely ignore the dying planet and refuse to send reinforcements.
The Reality: Space doesn't care about grid lines. A purely distance-based clustering system is always superior to rigid quadrants.

3. The "Bait and Punish" Assumption
We flag an enemy planet as "Vulnerable" the moment it launches a fleet.

The Exploit: What if the enemy has 1,000 ships on a planet, and they launch a tiny fleet of 5 ships? Our agent flags that planet as "Vulnerable" and blindly orders our Tanks to attack it. Our fleets will arrive expecting an empty planet and will instead crash into a wall of 995 ships, completely wiping out our army.
The Reality: We don't check how many ships the enemy has left before deciding they are vulnerable.

4. The Sun Blockade (Pathfinding Failure)
If the direct line between your planet and a target hits the Sun, your agent returns None and aborts the attack entirely.

The Exploit: If an enemy captures a planet on the exact opposite side of the Sun from your empire, your agent will literally just sit there. It does not know how to "route around" the sun by sending ships to a side-planet first, nor does it wait for rotation to clear the path.
The Reality: The enemy will quietly sit in the sun's shadow, building an infinite army while your agent throws its hands up in defeat.

5. The Target-Lock Paralysis
When we send a fleet, we lock the target self._targeted[tgt.id] = turns + 2 so we don't send multiple fleets to the same place.

The Exploit: What if the enemy reinforces that planet while our fleet is in the air? Our fleet will land, fail to capture the planet, and die. Because of the "Target Lock", our agent will refuse to send a follow-up attack for several turns, giving the enemy time to regenerate.
The Reality: The agent assumes every launch is guaranteed to succeed. It doesn't dynamically recalculate if an in-flight fleet is still strong enough to win.

6. Ignoring the Game Clock
The game ends at a specific step limit (usually 1000 steps). The winner is whoever has the most ships.

The Exploit: At step 980, your agent might launch a massive 95% fleet at an enemy planet that takes 25 turns to arrive. The game will end at step 1000 while your fleet is still floating in space.
The Reality: While fleets in space usually count toward your final score, if they were meant to destroy an enemy planet, they won't arrive in time to subtract from the enemy's score. The agent has no "end-game" awareness to turtle up and protect its score in the final 50 turns.
